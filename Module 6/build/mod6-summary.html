§1.1 · Derivative markets — the foundation
Derivative — a financial contract whose value is derived from the value of some underlying asset. The underlying may be a share, bond, commodity, currency, interest rate or index — or an outcome (weather, carbon emissions, heating and cooling days). Derivatives manage financial risk, exploit investment opportunities, and create synthetic asset class exposure.
Exam watch The primary purpose of derivative markets is the transfer of risk without the need to trade the underlying asset. Learn that phrase verbatim. Size of the global market: ≈ US$800 trillion, against ≈ US$23 trillion for every exchange-listed company in the United States combined.

The three main classes

  • Forwards / futures — priced off spot plus a basis
  • Options — priced off time, volatility, strike
  • Swaps — priced by equating the PV of a fixed and a variable stream

Each class has its own pricing mechanism — the exam likes matching the class to its price driver.

What moves a derivative's price

  • Price of the underlying
  • Time to maturity
  • Volatility
  • Interest rates
  • Dividends
  • Credit risk & counterparty risk
  • Market conditions — liquidity, depth, transaction costs
Framing Derivative pricing is a complex process requiring an understanding of the instruments, the mathematical models and market dynamics. It is a fundamental aspect of financial markets — institutions, traders and risk managers all use pricing models to make informed decisions.

§1.1.9 · OTC vs exchange-traded derivatives

The guide's side-by-side

Exchange-traded contracts have standard terms and trade on an organised derivatives exchange (a futures exchange). They are tradable financial instruments, marked to market (settled daily), with performance guaranteed by the exchange's clearing house, and are highly liquid with an active secondary market because every contract is standardised.
OTC contracts are customised transactions created by two parties through a telephone- and computer-linked dealer network. Financial institutions often act as market makers for the more commonly traded instruments, trades are typically much larger than in the exchange-traded market, and there is usually some default risk.

§1.1.4–1.1.5 · Benefits and criticisms of derivative markets

Benefits — three headings

Price discovery

Futures prices can be thought of as a forecast of future spot prices — though in reality they provide only a little more information than the spot price, and they do so efficiently. A futures price also indicates what price would be acceptable to avoid uncertainty.
For options, one characteristic of the underlying is volatility; option pricing models let you back out the volatility implied by the option price. Implied volatility is a good measure of general uncertainty in the market — a measure of fear.

Operational advantages

  • Lower transaction costs than transacting in the equivalent underlying
  • Greater liquidity than the underlying market
  • Short positions entered into easily

Market efficiency

When prices deviate from fundamental value, derivatives offer a low-cost way to exploit the mispricing — less capital, lower costs, shorting possible. Investors are far more willing to trade if they can manage their risks, raising participant numbers and therefore market liquidity.

Criticisms — two headings

Speculation and gambling

For hedging to work there must be speculators on the other side of the trade — and the more speculators the market attracts, the cheaper it becomes to hedge. Yet speculators are perceived poorly: short-term traders thought to engage in price manipulation and trading at extreme prices. Profit from short-term trading is taxed more heavily than long-term profit — a way of “punishing” the activity. Many view derivatives trading as legalised gambling, but gambling benefits only a limited number of participants and does not help society as a whole, whereas the derivatives market brings extensive benefits to the financial services industry.

Destabilisation and systemic risk

Opponents argue the operational benefits produce excessive speculative trading and therefore instability. Speculators use large amounts of leverage, exposing themselves and their creditors to high risk; defaults by speculators can lead to defaults by their creditors, and these chain-reaction events can be systemic.
The second criticism is complexity: when the models underpinning derivative pricing break down under sometimes irrational market behaviour, the model builders are blamed for failing to capture financial market reality.

Quotable Warren Buffett called derivatives “financial weapons of mass destruction”. The deck's framing: a leveraged instrument can not only reduce risk but create risk through enhanced uncovered exposure. The three legitimate purposes: hedge (contain upside and downside) · insure (retain the upside, limit the downside) · transform assets and liabilities to match future expectations.

§1.1.7 · Concepts and terminology (1 of 2)
Arbitrage — a trading strategy requiring no cash where there is some probability of making a profit without incurring the risk of a loss. The simultaneous purchase and sale of identical or equivalent financial assets to benefit from a discrepancy in their price relationship. A low-risk strategy linked to hedging (risk transference) and speculation (risk acceptance).
Type of arbitrageWhat it trades
True arbitrageTwo assets with an equal fair value trading at different prices.
Risk arbitrageAn asset currently priced at a value that will soon change.
Convertible arbitrageThe difference between a bond's conversion price and the current price of the underlying company's shares.
Triangular arbitrageThree or more currencies traded simultaneously.
Speculation — buying and selling assets to profit from price fluctuations over the short term, as opposed to buying for long-term gains (buy and hold) or income (dividends). Associated with day trading, it relies on market timing and implies a riskier proposition than investing. It can involve any tradable asset — shares, bonds, commodity futures, currencies, cryptocurrency, fine art, collectibles, real estate and financial derivatives. Speculators provide liquidity and help narrow the bid-ask spread.
Hedging — offsetting the price risk inherent in a spot market position by taking an equal but opposite position in the futures market. Risk is neutralised and transferred to someone for whom holding that risk would reduce an existing but opposite exposure. A counterparty without a pre-existing exposure — a speculator — accepts the risk to benefit from an anticipated price movement.
Strategies: options hedging (e.g. buy a put to protect against a share price decline) · currency hedging (buy or sell a foreign currency to offset exchange rate effects) · direct hedging (open a position in the opposite direction of an existing trade, e.g. a sell position in the same pair you are long).
Hedging is used by short- to mid-term traders and investors — not typically as part of a long-term strategy, because short-term price fluctuations have little impact on buy-and-hold investors.

§1.1.7 · Concepts and terminology (2 of 2)
Marking to market — the mark-to-market process, or margining, ensures that all counterparties meet their obligations under the futures contract. The initial margin determines the level of gearing obtained from entering into a futures contract.
Volatility — the degree of fluctuation (rate of change) in the price of a security, expressed mathematically as variance or standard deviation, and beta. The greater the volatility, the wider the fluctuations between successive prices over a given period. The volatility of security returns is the measurement used to define risk. High volatility means considerable risk and an increased probability of either a loss or a gain in a short period. Futures markets are traditionally more volatile than spot markets.
Short selling — a strategy where a security is borrowed and sold with a commitment to purchase and replace it later. A complex, high-risk strategy of selling a security not owned, used to capitalise on an expected decline in price, which can lead to substantial losses if the opposite occurs. There are many restrictions on selling short.
Short selling applies to the spot or cash market. “Going short” or “shorting an asset” applies to the derivatives market.
Market maker — a JSE member — for example the investment banks ABSA, RMB, Investec and Standard Bank — that always quotes bid and ask prices for selected instruments, thereby making or maintaining a market in them. Market makers must cover or hedge their exposure in the futures market with equivalent transactions in the spot market — they buy or sell the client's full long or short exposure in the spot market.

§1.2.6 · Forwards — types, settlement and credit risk
Forward contract — an over-the-counter derivative contract in which two parties agree that one party, the buyer, will purchase an underlying asset from the other party, the seller, at a later date at a fixed price they agree on when the contract is signed. The parties also agree the identity of the underlying, the number of units to be delivered and where delivery will occur. The contract is customised, and each party is subject to the possibility that the other will default.

The four types of forward

TypeDefinition
Equity forwardsBinding agreements to buy or sell a specific stock or the return on a share index at a fixed entry price at a fixed expiry date.
Fixed-income forwardsAn agreement to transact in a fixed-income security at a preset price at a future forward date. Value = bond price − PV of coupon payments − PV of the price at expiration.
Interest rate forwards
also called FRAs		Forecasts that estimate future interest rates, used to plan for economic shifts. Calculated from the spot rate — the rate starting today — adjusted for the cost of carry.
Currency forwards
forward exchange contracts, FECs		A binding agreement that locks in an exchange rate for a future currency transaction. Privately negotiated and traded OTC in the foreign exchange market.

Settling a forward at expiration

Delivery — the long party pays the agreed-upon price; the short party delivers the underlying asset.

Cash settlement — the net position is settled:
the long receives money when Spot > Future
the short receives money when Future > Spot

The parties

The party that agrees to buy the asset has a long forward position. The party that agrees to sell has a short forward position.

Dealers are financial institutions quoting bid (buy) and ask (sell) prices, earning the spread. End users are companies or institutions managing risk — locking in a future price.

Credit risk in forward contracts There is credit risk because the counterparty may not deliver the asset at the time of delivery. And because a forward is not exchange traded, a buyer or seller cannot lock in gains or losses on the contract's value before the agreed settlement date — the contract must be held to maturity. Given this, the contract's value serves as the quantification of credit risk for the counterparties.

§1.2.7 · Futures — standardisation and the clearinghouse
Futures contract — a standardised derivative contract created and traded on a futures exchange in which two parties agree that one party, the buyer, will purchase an underlying asset from the other party, the seller, at a later date and at a price agreed on by the two parties when the contract is initiated, and in which there is a daily settling of gains and losses and a credit guarantee by the futures exchange through its clearinghouse.
Exam watch The most important distinctive characteristic of a futures contract is the daily settlement of gains and losses and the associated credit guarantee provided by the exchange through its clearinghouse. Learn the full definition above — the three elements in bold are where the marks are.

What standardisation buys you

  • Futures are specialised versions of forward contracts that have been standardised and trade on a futures exchange
  • Standardised contracts + an organised market with rules and regulations + a central clearing facility deliver liquidity and protection against loss by default
  • A futures contract requires a futures exchange — a legally recognised entity providing a market for trading these contracts
  • Futures exchanges are highly regulated at the national level in all countries, and specify that only certain contracts are authorised for trading
  • Those contracts have specific underlying assets, times to expiration, delivery and settlement conditions and quantities
  • The exchange offers a physical location and/or electronic system, plus liquidity provided by authorised market makers
  • Futures are guaranteed by the clearinghouse — no counterparty risk; gains and losses are settled daily (marked to market); and margin is required as collateral to cover losses
Contrast with forwards In a forward contract the parties customise everything — the underlying, time to expiration, delivery and settlement conditions and quantity — according to whatever terms they agree. These contracts are not traded on an exchange, and although OTC regulation is increasing, they are not subject to the high degree of regulation that applies to securities and futures markets.

Like forward markets, futures markets can be used for hedging or speculation — for example a jewellery manufacturer can buy gold futures, hedging the price it will have to pay for one of its key inputs.

§1.2.7 · Mark to market, futures pricing and payoff shape

Mark to market — the daily settlement

The agreed-upon price is the futures price. Identical contracts trade on an ongoing basis at different prices, reflecting the passage of time and the arrival of new information — so as the futures price changes, the parties make or lose money. Rising prices benefit the long and hurt the short; falling prices do the reverse.

At the end of each day the clearinghouse determines an average of the final futures trades of the day and designates that price the settlement price. All contracts are then marked to the settlement price.

Worked example — the guide's own

The long purchases the contract during the day at a futures price of R120. The settlement price at the end of the day is R122. The long's account is marked for a gain of R2 — that amount is credited to his account, with the money coming from the account of the short, who has lost R2. If the futures price decreases instead, the long loses money and is charged with that loss, and the money is transferred to the account of the short.

Futures pricing — the class deck's formula

F = S (1 + r)t

Deck worked example: spot R120, rate 8%, one year → F = 120(1.08)¹ = R129.60. This is the cost-of-carry relationship — the futures price is the spot price carried forward at the financing rate, which is why the guide describes a futures price as “the spot price along with a basis amount”.

Payoff shape

A futures contract is an obligation, so it exhibits a symmetrical payoff — gain and loss run in both directions without limit. Options, being rights, are asymmetrical: the buyer's loss is capped at the premium.

Hedge profiles

Long futures locks in a specific purchase price.
Short futures locks in a specific selling price.
Both eliminate any downside (loss) — but also forfeit any upside (gain).

Exam watch Mark to market is the mechanism that makes futures credit-safe: the gain of one party is funded daily out of the account of the other, so no large unsettled exposure accumulates — which is precisely what a forward contract does allow.

§1.3 · Options — definition and concepts
Option — a derivative contract in which one party, the buyer, pays a sum of money to the other party, the seller or writer, and receives the right to either buy or sell an underlying asset at a fixed price, either on a specific expiration date or at any time prior to it. The purchase price of the option is the premium; sellers of options receive premium income.

Call option

Grants the holder (long position) the opportunity to buy the underlying security at a price below the current market price, provided the market price exceeds the call strike before or at expiration.

Put option

Grants the holder (long position) the opportunity to sell the underlying security at a price above the current market price, provided the put strike exceeds the market price before or at expiration.

In both cases the option seller (short position) receives a payment — the premium — compelling performance at the discretion of the holder. If the holder exercises, the writer must make delivery (call) or take delivery (put). Combinations of options lead to different exposures; the incentive to contract as long or short centres on perceptions regarding market direction and expectations.

The six options concepts

TermDefinition
European-style optionAn option that can be exercised and settled only on its expiration date.
American-style optionAn option that can be exercised and settled on or before its expiration date — the most common type.
MoneynessThe degree to which an option is in the money and likely to be exercised, thereby having a payoff.
PayoffThe difference between the spot and strike prices when exercised.
Breakeven priceThe spot price at which the holder breaks even — zero profit — after accounting for the cost of obtaining the option.
Profit or lossThe payoff minus the cost of obtaining the option (the premium).

Moneyness tells the holder whether exercising will lead to a profit, looking at the value of the option if you were to exercise it right away. Guide example: a call to buy XYZ at R40 while XYZ trades at R45 is in the money — you could exercise to buy at a discount. Intrinsic value and time value

The option premium breaks into two components — intrinsic value and speculative or time value (extrinsic value).

Market Price − Strike Price
= intrinsic value of a call
Strike Price − Market Price
= intrinsic value of a put

In both cases multiply by the number of shares in the contract — typically one hundred. Intrinsic value represents the profit the holder would book if they exercised, took delivery and sold the asset in the current marketplace.

Time value Time value is harder to quantify. The more time before expiry, the more opportunity for the option's moneyness to increase — so time value is higher the longer to expiration, and reduces over time until, at expiration, it reaches R0. American-type options can be exercised at any point up to that date.

Worked example 1 — buying a call (guide §1.2.3)

One call option contract on ABC covers 100 shares. Strike R25, premium paid R150. On expiration date ABC shares are selling for R35.

Cost of exercising — 100 × R25R2,500
Sale proceeds — 100 × R35R3,500
Gross profit on the option — R3,500 − R2,500R1,000
Less the premium paid−R150

A very good return on a R150 investment. The buyer's maximum loss is always limited to the premium and the upside is unlimited — the guide calls this asymmetry “one of the most attractive features of buying options”.

Worked example 2 — breakeven on a call (class deck)

An IBM share trades at R100. An April call has a premium of R5 and an exercise price of R100. Ignoring commissions:

Breakeven = Strike + Premium = R100 + R5 = R105

The share price must rise above R105 for the option holder to earn a profit. Between R100 and R105 the option is in the money but the holder is still down on the premium; below R100 it expires worthless and the loss is the R5 premium.

§1.3.2 · Hedging with options · writing calls

Worked example 3 — the protective put

An investor owns 1,000 Sasol shares priced at R28. He is concerned about a possible decline over the next two months and wants protection. He buys ten July R27.50 put option contracts, giving him the right to sell 1,000 shares at R27.50. The put premium is R1 — R100 cost per put.

Total cost of the hedging strategy — 10 × R100R1,000
Proceeds if exercised — 1,000 × R27.50R27,500
Amount realised once the cost of the options is taken into accountR26,500

The strategy guarantees the shares can be sold for R27.50 during the life of the option. If Sasol stays above R27.50, the options expire worthless and the R1,000 was the cost of the insurance.

The four principles of hedging with options

  • Hedging means taking a position in some market that will limit or protect the downside in another position
  • Calls and puts allow a great deal of flexibility in creating a hedge
  • Protective puts establish a downside floor; selling a call against an existing position generates income while limiting upside potential
  • Hedging with options incurs a cost — understand the risks and rewards of each position

Writing calls — covered vs naked

TypePosition and risk
Covered callThe seller actually owns the underlying stock. Selling calls on stock you own produces additional income and offsets expected declines in the price. The seller is “covered” against a loss — if the buyer exercises, he delivers shares he already owns. His profit is limited to the stock's rise to the strike price, but he is protected against any actual loss.
Naked callThe seller does not own the underlying. Very risky — there is no limit to how high a share price can go, and the seller is not covered. On exercise the naked seller must buy the stock at the current market price to deliver; the difference between market price and strike is his loss. Most option sellers charge a high fee to compensate.
The writer's side generally The call seller's downside is potentially unlimited — as spot exceeds the strike the writer's loss equals the buyer's profit. If the market price never goes higher than the strike, the option expires worthless and the seller keeps the premium. Deck example: a call on RBC with a R10 premium and a R100 strike — the buyer loses R10 if RBC does not rise past R100; the writer is in the money as long as the share stays below R110. For the equivalent put, the writer is in the money while the share stays above R90.

§1.4 · Swaps — definition and interest rate swaps
Swap — an over-the-counter derivative contract in which two parties agree to exchange a series of cash flows whereby one party pays a variable series determined by an underlying asset or rate and the other pays either (1) a variable series determined by a different underlying asset or rate, or (2) a fixed series.

Key characteristics

Cash flows — one is fixed, the other variable, based on an index price, interest rate or exchange rate. Principal — the principal amount usually does not change hands during or at the end of the swap. Trading — OTC or on security-based Swap Execution Facilities (SEFs). Types — the most common are interest rate and currency swaps.

Where a swap sits

A swap is more like a forward contract — a series of forward contracts — than a futures contract: it is an OTC contract, privately negotiated and subject to default. Other terms — the identity of the underlying, payment dates and payment procedure — are negotiated between the parties and written into the contract.

Interest rate swap — a forward contract in which one stream of future interest payments is exchanged for another based on a specified principal amount. Usually a fixed rate payment is exchanged for a floating rate payment, or vice versa, to reduce or increase exposure to interest rate fluctuations, or to obtain a marginally lower interest rate than would otherwise be possible. Also called plain vanilla swaps, being the original and simplest such instruments.

The three types of interest rate swap

TypeStructure
Fixed-to-floatingA company that can issue a bond at an attractive fixed rate but wants better cash flow from a floating rate enters a swap in which it receives a fixed rate and pays a floating rate. The swap is structured to match the maturity and cash flow of the fixed-rate bond, and the two payment streams are netted. The company receives SOFR plus or minus a spread reflecting both market interest rate conditions and its credit rating.
Floating-to-fixedA company with no access to a fixed-rate loan borrows at a floating rate and enters a swap to acquire a fixed rate. The loan's floating-rate tenor, reset and payment dates are mirrored on the swap and netted, and the fixed-rate leg of the swap becomes the company's borrowing rate.

§1.4.4–1.4.6 · Currency swaps, swaptions and CDS

The five motivations for a swap

Hedging against interest rate risk — convert fixed-rate debt into floating, or vice versa · customising cash flow to align with financial goals · accessing new markets and rates otherwise unavailable · optimising debt structure · converting foreign currency payments into local currency for bonds issued abroad.
As in most financial transactions, a swap dealer sits between the two parties and takes a commission on the trade. Deck example of a plain vanilla swap: Company A pays LIBOR + 1.1% while Bank B pays a fixed 4.7%.

Currency swaps

Guide example: Party A pays Party B $10 million in return for €9.8 million. On each settlement date Party A, who received euros, pays 6% interest in euros on €9.8 million, and Party B pays 5% interest on the $10 million received. There is no netting of payments — they are in different currencies.

Motivation: a company wants to establish operations in a foreign country and finance the costs in that currency, but it is more expensive to issue debt in the foreign country. It issues debt in its local currency and enters a currency swap to exchange for the foreign currency; the counterparty faces a similar situation in reverse, and an intermediary swap dealer facilitates. Currency swaps also let a company transform a foreign currency bond into a domestic obligation — a receive-fixed foreign currency, pay fixed or floating domestic currency swap — and likewise transform a foreign investment into a domestic one.

The four possible types: pay fixed on foreign / receive fixed on local · pay floating on foreign / receive fixed on local · pay fixed on foreign / receive floating on local · pay floating on foreign / receive floating on local.

Swaption — an option on a swap: an option to enter into a swap at a fixed rate. The most widely used is the plain vanilla interest rate swaption. It lets the holder establish a fixed rate on the underlying swap in advance and then choose either to enter the swap at that rate or let the swaption expire and enter at the prevailing market rate.
Payer swaption — an option on a swap to pay fixed and receive floating; the buyer hopes the fixed rate goes up before expiry. Receiver swaption — an option to receive fixed and pay floating; the buyer hopes the fixed rate goes down.
Two motivations: to change the pattern of payments on an upcoming fixed-income obligation — a payer swaption protects against a rate increase, and if the market rate at expiry is below the contract rate the buyer walks away and pays the lower market rate — and to terminate a swap position, the swaption acting as an insurance policy if the original swap goes wrong. As with all options, the buyer pays a premium for this asymmetric payoff — the right to walk away.
Credit default swap (CDS) A credit derivative contract enabling investors to swap credit risk on a company, country or other entity with another counterparty. The most common type of OTC credit derivative, often used to transfer credit exposure on fixed income products in order to hedge risk. If a lender fears a borrower will default, it buys a CDS from another investor who agrees to reimburse it if the borrower defaults. Most CDS require an ongoing premium payment to maintain the contract — like an insurance policy.

The partnership structure

A partnership structure is common for many alternative investments — hedge funds, infrastructure funds and private equity funds. The fund manager is the general partner (GP) and investors are limited partners (LPs).

General partner (GP)

The fund manager. Runs the business and theoretically bears unlimited liability for anything that might go wrong.

Limited partners (LPs)

The investors. Own a fractional interest in the partnership based on their investment amount and the terms of the partnership documentation.

Limited partnerships are restricted to investors expected to understand and to be able to assume the risks. Because they are not offered to the general public they are less regulated than public offerings. Four terms you must be able to define and distinguish. They recur in the hedge fund chapter and carry the calculation marks.

§2.2 · Hedge funds — what they are
Hedge fund — an investment fund that pools capital from individual or institutional investors, administered by a professional investment management firm, investing across assets ranging from vanilla to complex. A contemporary hedge fund is an aggressively managed portfolio of investments across asset classes and regions that is leveraged, takes long and short positions, and/or uses derivatives, with a goal of generating high returns — either in an absolute sense or over a specified market benchmark — and few, if any, investment restrictions.

Structural characteristics

  • Set up as a private investment partnership open to a limited number of investors willing and able to make a large initial investment
  • Restrictions on redemptions — investors may be required to keep money in the fund for a minimum period, the lockup period, before they may withdraw or redeem shares
  • Investors may be required to give notice of intent to redeem — typically 30 to 90 days — and may be charged a fee to redeem shares
  • Managers typically invest a significant amount of personal capital — in some cases in excess of 50% of the total assets in the fund — aligning their interests with those of investors
  • Investors share the partnership's income, expenses, gains and losses, each partner taxed according to their respective share
  • Hedge funds aim to consistently achieve positive returns under all market conditions, and do not necessarily hedge all risk as the name might imply

Investment manager

Determines strategy, makes investing decisions and allocations, and manages portfolio risk. The manager is also invested in the fund and is compensated via a management fee plus a performance fee based on the fund's annual performance. Managers only get a performance fee if the fund makes money above a certain benchmark.

Prime broker

Funds must secure their loans with collateral to gain margin and execute trades. Each broker — usually a large securities firm — uses its own risk matrix to determine how much to lend each client. Brokers are themselves subject to regulations, which act as indirect regulations on the hedge funds doing business with them.

Funds of hedge funds Funds that hold a portfolio of hedge funds, creating a diversified portfolio accessible to smaller investors or to those without the resources, time or expertise to choose among managers. They are assumed to have expertise in conducting due diligence and may be able to negotiate better redemption terms than individual investors. They may diversify across strategies, investment regions and management styles. The distinction between a single hedge fund and a fund of funds is not necessarily clear-cut — hedge funds can invest in other hedge funds.

§2.2.4–2.2.7 · Benefits, due diligence, valuation and SA hedge funds

Potential benefits and risks

Hedge fund returns have tended to be better than global equities in down markets and to lag global equities in up markets. Different strategies have the best returns during different time periods. Statements about performance and diversification benefits are problematic because of the great variety of strategies used. Less-than-perfect correlation with global equity returns may offer some diversification benefit, but correlations tend to increase during periods of financial crisis.
Institutional investors' number one objective is returns uncorrelated to equity. Correlation with traditional stocks runs 20–35% for futures, fixed arbitrage and global macro funds, up to 70–80% for long/short or emerging equity funds.

Valuation

Values are based on market values for traded securities but must use model (estimated) values for non-traded securities. It is most conservative to use the prices at which a position could be closed — bid prices for long positions and ask prices for short positions; some funds use the average of bid and ask instead. For illiquid securities, quoted prices may be reduced for the degree of illiquidity, based on position size relative to total value outstanding and average trading volume.

Due diligence — what to examine

Investment strategy · investment process · source of competitive advantages · historical returns · valuation and returns calculation methods · longevity · amount of assets under management · management style · key person risk · reputation · growth plans · systems for risk management · appropriateness of benchmarks.
The analysis is challenging: a lack of persistence in returns means past outperformers may not outperform in future, and reputation, risk systems and management style are difficult to quantify. Previously profitable strategies become less profitable as more funds pursue them. One further factor: the percentage of total AUM held by any single investor — a large withdrawal may cause the fund to fail, especially if assets are illiquid. Transparency is limited: funds treat strategies as proprietary and regulatory disclosure requirements are minimal.

Internationally hedge funds have been around about 60 years; in SA they are a relatively newer phenomenon. Following the development of securities lending in the early 1990s, some hedge trading developed in proprietary funds. By 2000 there were approximately 20–30 hedge funds (R2.5bn). Drivers for growth: regulatory development — Regulation 28, covering pension fund investment into hedge funds, and regulation of hedge funds under CISCA as either a Retail Investor Hedge Fund or a Qualified Investor Hedge Fund — and demand from international investors for diversified investment exposure.

§2.4 · Real estate — the asset types

Investment in real estate can provide income in the form of rents as well as the potential for capital gains. As an asset class it can provide diversification benefits and a potential inflation hedge, because rents and values tend to increase with inflation. The three asset types: residential property (single-family homes), commercial property (produces income), and mortgages (loans with residential or commercial property as collateral).

Residential property

Considered a direct investment in real estate. Some buyers pay cash but most take a mortgage. The issuer (lender) of the mortgage has a direct investment in a whole loan and is said to “hold the mortgage”. Issuers often sell the mortgages they originate; those are then pooled (securitised) as publicly traded mortgage-backed securities (MBS), which represent an indirect investment in the mortgage loan pool. Property purchased with a mortgage is a leveraged investment, and the owner's equity is the property value minus the outstanding loan amount — so changes in property value over time affect the owner's equity.

Commercial real estate

Generates income from rents. Homes purchased for rental income are considered investment in commercial property. Large properties such as an office building are a direct investment for institutions or wealthy individuals, purchased for cash or leveraged with a mortgage. Long time horizons, illiquidity, the large size of investment needed and the complexity of the investments make commercial real estate inappropriate for many investors. It can also be held by a limited partnership — partners have limited liability and the general partner manages the investment and the properties — or by a REIT.

REIT — real estate investment trust — issues shares that trade publicly like shares of stock. REITs are often identified by the type of real estate assets they hold: mortgages, hotel properties, malls, office buildings or other commercial property. Income is used to pay dividends, and typically 90% of income must be distributed to shareholders to avoid taxes that the REIT would otherwise pay before distribution.
Why REITs exist The REIT market has expanded to overcome many of the problems of direct investment. Shares are actively traded and are more likely to reflect market value; investing in a REIT gives exposure to a diversified real estate portfolio; and investors do not need property management expertise, because the REIT manages the properties.

Difficulty in determining priceHeterogeneity and low transaction volume mean appraisals are usually necessary — and even then values are often based on similar, not identical, properties. Limited market participants and lack of local knowledge make it hard for an outsider to value property, so the market is less efficient — which means investors with superior information and skill may have an advantage in exploiting the inefficiencies.
The pay-off for that inefficiency Because appraisals rest on similar rather than identical properties and few participants know a local market well, real estate is less efficiently priced than listed securities — which is exactly why investors with superior information and skill may have an advantage in exploiting the market inefficiencies.

§2.4.2–2.4.3 · Real estate — reasons to invest and principal risks

Five reasons to invest

  • Current income — rents, after operating expenses, financing costs and taxes
  • Capital appreciation — property values expected to rise over time
  • Inflation hedge — both rents and values expected to rise with inflation
  • Diversification — less than perfectly correlated with stocks and bonds
  • Tax benefits — in some countries the depreciable life is shorter than the actual life, so depreciation expense is higher and taxable income lower; and REITs do not pay taxes in some countries, letting investors escape double taxation

The principal risks

Business conditions — GDP, employment, household income, interest rates, inflation · new property lead time — conditions can weaken between approval, completion and full lease-up · cost and availability of capital · unexpected inflation — some leases pass it through, but values may not keep up when markets are weak · demographic factors · lack of liquidity — a quick sale requires a significant discount · environmental issues — contamination by a prior or adjacent owner · availability of information · management expertise · leverage — measured by the loan-to-value (LTV) ratio; higher LTV means higher risk since lenders have a superior claim on default, and a small decrease in NOI is magnified in the cash flow left to equity · other — defects, natural disasters, terrorism.
Identified risks can be hedged using insurance or shifted to tenants — a lease could require the tenant to reimburse unexpected operating expenses.

Exam watch Two real estate traps recur. First, homes purchased for rental income are an investment in commercial property, not residential — the classification follows the income, not the building type. Second, on leverage: a higher LTV means higher risk because lenders hold a superior claim in the event of default, and because a small fall in net operating income is magnified in whatever cash flow reaches the equity holder after debt service.

§2.5 · Commodities — routes to exposure

Commodities are real assets, mostly natural resources — agricultural products, oil, natural gas, precious and industrial metals. They are considered a hedge against inflation as they are not sensitive to public equity markets, and their value rises and falls with supply and demand. From politics and weather to disease and speculation, commodities are among the most volatile instruments in the financial world.

Why derivatives dominate While it is possible to invest directly in commodities such as grain and gold, the most commonly used instruments to gain exposure to commodity prices are derivatives — because commodities are physical goods and therefore incur costs for storage and transportation. Returns are based on price changes and not on income streams.
RouteNote
Direct physical investmentUnless the investment comprises commodities such as Krugerrand gold coins, investors would require warehouses to store the commodities — generally considered impractical and uneconomic. Physical investment is also limiting for investors seeking broad exposure.
Shares of commodity producersExposes the investor not only to commodities but to the financial structure, governance and management skills and practices of the company. In addition the company may validly hedge its own production, so investors will not get the full benefit of changes in commodity prices.
Commodity futuresPurchase futures contracts to simulate ownership. By periodically rolling over the contracts prior to expiry and reinvesting in new futures, investors obtain returns equal to the return from a single commodity or an index of several.
Commodity index fundsLet investors buy a balanced and diversified basket of commodities in a single investment. Index funds do not have physical ownership — they use futures to buy a forward position, sell it as it approaches expiry, and use the proceeds to buy forward again by one or two months. The most liquid index is the Standard & Poor's Goldman Sachs Commodities Index (S&P GSCI).
ETFs and ETNsExchange traded funds (ETFs) are traded instruments representing ownership in an underlying portfolio of commodities. Exchange traded notes (ETNs) are traded as debt securities — a promise to pay the investor an amount linked to a given benchmark, so the investor takes on the credit risk of the issuer. There are currently no commodity-based ETFs or ETNs in South Africa.
Exchange traded commodities (ETCs)Vehicles that track the performance of a single underlying commodity — gold, oil, zinc — or a commodity index. Traded and settled exactly like normal shares, with dedicated ETC sectors on the London Stock Exchange, Euronext Amsterdam and Deutsche Börse. Generally benchmark a total-return index. The first ETC was a Gold ETC listed in 2003 on the Australian Securities Exchange; an ETC was first listed on the LSE in 2004.

§2.5.1–2.5.5 · Sectors, investors, valuation and returns

The six sectors

Energy — crude oil, natural gas and refined products; the sector with the greatest market value. Industrial metals — aluminium, nickel, zinc, lead, tin, iron and copper, plus rare-earth metals used increasingly in electronics. Grains — wheat, corn, soybeans, rice. Livestock — pigs, sheep, cattle, poultry. Precious metals — gold, silver, platinum. Softs (cash crops) — coffee, sugar, cocoa, cotton.

The three types of investor

Institutional — insurers, banks, pension funds, investment companies, collective investment schemes, hedge funds and endowments; most hold commodities as a non-essential diversifying investment. Private — high net worth and sophisticated individuals, for an inflation hedge and diversification. Retail — limited direct exposure, commodities traditionally regarded as too volatile; internationally indirect exposure comes through the assets institutions hold on their behalf, such as pension funds.

Two sector dynamics worth knowing

Crude oil can be stored indefinitely by keeping it in the ground, and in tanks and tanker ships; many countries hold strategic reserves. The two most traded grades are Brent Crude and West Texas Intermediate (WTI). Supply is affected by drilling and extraction technology, economic cycles, alternative energy efficiency, environmental restrictions and political risk — over half the crude supply comes from the Middle East.
Industrial metals demand is primarily tied to GDP growth and business cycles, through construction and manufacturing. Storage of metals is not costly. Union strikes and environmental regulation can significantly affect supply; mines and smelters are large-scale operations with high development and fixed costs.

§2.5.4–2.5.5 · Commodity prices, valuation and the sources of return

Spot prices for commodities are a function of supply and demand. Demand is affected by the value of the commodity to end-users and by global economic conditions and cycles. Supply is affected by production and storage costs and existing inventories. Both supply and demand are affected by the purchases and sales of non-hedging investors — speculators.
For many commodities supply is inelastic in the short run, because of the long lead times to alter production levels — drilling oil wells, planting crops, or deciding to plant less of them. As a result commodity prices can be volatile when demand changes significantly over the economic cycle. Production of some commodities, especially agricultural, can be significantly affected by the weather — high prices when production is low, low prices when production is high. Costs of extracting oil and minerals increase as more expensive methods or more remote areas are used.

What each side analyses

Producers analyse economic events, government policy and forecasts of future supply to estimate future needs. Investors analyse inventory levels, forecasts of production, changes in government policy and expectations of economic growth in order to forecast commodity prices.

Why the formula has the shape it does Wheat today and wheat six months from today are different products. Buying the commodity today gives the buyer the use of it if needed; contracting for delivery in six months avoids storage costs and having cash tied up. The cost-of-carry equation prices exactly that difference.

Valuation — the cost-of-carry equation

futures price = spot price (1 + risk-free rate) + storage costs − convenience yield

Contango vs backwardation

Convenience yield is the value of having the physical commodity for use over the period of the futures contract. If this equation does not hold, an arbitrage transaction is possible.
Little or no convenience yield → futures higher than spot → contango.
High convenience yield → futures less than spot → backwardation.

Three sources of futures returns

Roll yield — from the difference between spot and futures, or between two futures of different expiry, as futures converge toward spot near expiration. Positive for a market in backwardation, negative for a market in contango. Collateral yield — the interest earned on collateral required to enter the futures contract. Change in spot prices — total price return combines the spot move and the convergence of futures to spot.

Benefits and risks Returns have been lower than global stocks or bonds and Sharpe ratios low. But correlations with global equities and bonds are historically low — typically less than 0.2, so commodities diversify; and because prices move with inflation they hedge inflation risk. To the extent they track inflation the real return would be zero, though futures may offer positive real returns. Supply is inelastic in the short run — long lead times to drill or plant — which is why prices are volatile over the cycle.

§2.6 · Cryptocurrency and blockchain
Blockchain — a time-stamped series of an immutable record of data managed by a cluster of computers that are not owned by a central authority. A blockchain is transparent, and everyone involved is accountable for their actions. Blockchain is the technology that enables the existence of cryptocurrency, among other things.
Cryptocurrency — a digital or virtual currency secured by cryptography; cryptocurrencies can also be thought of as decentralised networks based on blockchain technology. A cryptocurrency is a medium of exchange, such as the US dollar, but digital, using encryption techniques to control the creation of monetary units and to verify the transfer of funds.

Why it became an alternative investment

Cryptocurrency has become an increasingly popular alternative investment thanks to its relative accessibility. Because it is based on blockchain networks used for security, it is almost impossible to double-spend cryptocurrency or for it to be counterfeit. It also operates entirely independently from the government, making it unaffected by interference.
The drawbacks: that same independence has in some cases led to its use in illegal activities, and while it is thought to be extremely secure, the networks are still subject to some vulnerabilities.
There are over 800 different cryptocurrencies currently on the market according to Coin Market Cap, a platform where cryptocurrencies are ranked based on their total market volume. The most well-known example is Bitcoin, which is also the most valuable today.

Bitcoin defined

  • The first decentralised digital currency and worldwide payment system, created by a slow computer process called mining
  • The international currency is not regulated by banks and can be used to purchase goods from anywhere in the world
  • There is only 21 million Bitcoin that can be mined in total, and this creates demand as adoption increases
  • It is decentralised digital cash for the internet, not controlled by a central government or federal reserve, but by the people who invest in it
  • All transactions are processed by miners, creating a digital ledger of secure and mostly anonymous transactions between individuals

BenefitMechanism
FraudCryptocurrencies are digital and cannot be counterfeited or reversed arbitrarily by the sender, as with credit card chargebacks.

Future scenarios the guide names

Bitcoin, Ethereum, Ripple, Litecoin and others continue to experience massive growth in price, market capitalisation and mainstream adoption. It is no longer a question of whether cryptocurrencies are disrupting the global economy, but by how much and what the future holds.
